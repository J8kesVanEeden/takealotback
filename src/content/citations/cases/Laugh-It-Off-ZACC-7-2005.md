# Laugh It Off Promotions CC v South African Breweries International (Finance) BV t/a SAB Mark International
## [2005] ZACC 7 | Constitutional Court | 27 May 2005 | Moseneke J

## Why this case is the legal-grounding anchor for TakealotBack

The Constitutional Court here drew the line between trade-mark anti-dilution claims under TMA s 34(1)(c) and the constitutional right to freedom of expression under s 16. It is the authority the site relies on for the proposition that a trade-mark owner cannot use s 34(1)(c) against expressive use, including criticism, without proving likely substantial economic detriment. The case was about parody T-shirts that the applicant "sells … for profit in order to make social comment"; it was not about domain names, and the site's domain-name position rests separately on UDRP para 4(c)(iii).

(Corrected 2026-11-18: this paragraph and the description said the case "protects non-commercial expressive use" and lets a criticism site use a mark "as part of its domain name". The T-shirts were sold for profit, and the case did not concern domain names.)

The court did **not** hold that any expressive use defeats trade-mark rights. It held that the proprietor must prove a specific kind of harm at a specific level before s 34(1)(c) can be invoked against expressive use. That threshold is the "substantial economic detriment" test below.

## The substantial-economic-detriment test (Moseneke J)

Our paraphrase, not a quotation: a proprietor invoking the anti-dilution provision in s 34(1)(c) against expressive use must prove the likelihood of substantial economic detriment to the mark. Offence to the proprietor or unauthorised use is not enough, and the onus is on the proprietor.

The court's own words (verbatim, SAFLII text re-checked 2026-11-17):

> [49] "… Equally clear is that the detriment relied upon must not be flimsy or negligible. It must be substantial in the sense that it is likely to cause substantial harm to the uniqueness and repute of the marks. … A court will have to weigh carefully the competing interests of the owner of the mark against the claim of free expression of a user without permission."

> [50] "… In sum, in order to succeed the owner of the mark bears the onus to demonstrate likelihood of substantial harm or detriment which, seen within the context of the case, amounts to unfairness."

> [56] "I hold that in a claim under section 34(1)(c), a party that seeks to oust an expressive conduct protected under the Constitution must, on the facts, establish a likelihood of substantial economic detriment to the claimant's mark. There is indeed much to be said for the contention that, in a claim based on tarnishment of a trade mark, the probability of material detriment to the mark envisaged in the section must be restricted to economic and trade harm. In essence the protection is against detriment to the repute of the mark; and not against the dignity but the selling magnetism of the mark. In an open democracy valuable expressive acts in public ought not to be lightly trampled upon by marginal detriment or harm unrelated to the commercial value that vests in the mark itself."

(Paragraph numbers from SAFLII's numbered list. An earlier version of this file set the paraphrase out as a blockquote, which read as a quotation. Corrected 2026-11-17: the paraphrase is now plain text and the court's words are quoted separately.)

Where the use is constitutionally protected speech under s 16 of the Constitution (including parody, criticism, commentary, and similar expressive use) the court must conduct a constitutional balancing exercise: the proprietor's commercial interest in the integrity of its mark on the one side; the speaker's right to engage in expressive use on the other.

Where the proprietor has not made out the "substantial economic detriment" case, the expressive use is protected.

## Facts

Laugh It Off Promotions printed T-shirts that copied the look of SAB's CARLING BLACK LABEL beer trade marks and changed the words: "Black Label" became "Black Labour" and "Carling Beer" became "White Guilt". SAB obtained a High Court interdict on s 34(1)(c) anti-dilution grounds. The SCA upheld the interdict. The Constitutional Court reversed.

Verbatim (same SAFLII capture; paragraph numbers not given because the capture's text drops SAFLII's list numbering):

> "… The words “Black Label” on the respondent's registered trade marks were replaced, on the T-shirt, with “Black Labour”; the respondent's “Carling Beer” was substituted with “White Guilt”; and where written “America's lusty lively beer” and “enjoyed by men around the world”, the applicant had printed “Africa's lusty lively exploitation since 1652” and “No regard given worldwide”, respectively."

> "The applicant sells the T-shirts for profit in order to make social comment."

> "The respondent approached the High Court for an interdict on the ground that the unauthorised use of its registered trade marks by the applicant in the course of trade offended the anti-dilution provisions of section 34(1)(c). …"

> "The High Court upheld the respondent's claim and granted with costs the restraint order sought. …"

> "The SCA dismissed the appeal on several grounds. …"

> Order: "The appeal against the judgment and order of the Supreme Court of Appeal succeeds. The order of the Supreme Court of Appeal is set aside. The order of the High Court is set aside and replaced by the following order: “Application dismissed”. …"

## Site reliance

The About section's "Legal grounding" list names and links the case for the s 34(1)(c) threshold, and the FAQ on using "Takealot" in the domain name names it directly. Both rely on it for one proposition: a proprietor invoking the anti-dilution provision, s 34(1)(c), against expressive use must prove a likelihood of substantial economic detriment to the mark (paras 50 and 56 quoted above), and that claim is weighed against the constitutional right to expression (para 49: a court "will have to weigh carefully the competing interests of the owner of the mark against the claim of free expression"). The FAQ adds that mere offence is not enough. The holding is limited to claims under s 34(1)(c) (para 56).
