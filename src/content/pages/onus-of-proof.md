# Who has to prove what

This is probably the most useful single page on the site. Get this right and every Takealot refusal looks different.

## Inside the first 6 months (CPA s56 window)

When you return goods under section 56 of the Consumer Protection Act within 6 months of delivery, you do not have to prove the goods were defective from the moment they left the warehouse. You only have to show that they fail the section 55 quality standard *now*, while still inside the window.

The practical burden then sits with the supplier to rebut. They have to show that the defect did not exist at delivery, or that it arose from your misuse, unauthorised alteration, or a recognised exclusion (normal wear and tear, electrical surge, consumer damage, sea-air corrosion, consumer modification, or unintended use).

The courts have now said this, at least where problems show up soon after delivery. In *Edan Traders v National Consumer Tribunal* [2026] ZAGPJHC 386, a Full Court of the Gauteng Division held that where the consumer shows problems arose shortly after delivery, the supplier carries the onus to rebut the defect and to prove the problems came from the consumer's own conduct: "The onus on the purchaser is to demonstrate that there were problems, not to show the cause of the problems." It followed *Toyota Randburg v Ndlovu* [2024] ZAGPPHC 591, which held that putting that burden on the consumer "would also be unfair on the consumer and would go against the object and purpose of the CPA especially where there is a need for in-depth knowledge that a consumer would ordinarily not have." And in *Wingfield Motors v National Consumer Tribunal* [2025] ZAWCHC 385, the Western Cape High Court (the forum Takealot's own T&Cs choose) rejected a dealer's claim that the customer's driving caused the defects because the dealer "did not present any evidence to support that claim but merely speculated."

All three were vehicle cases. The reasoning, that a consumer can't be expected to have the technical knowledge to prove why something failed, applies with more force to a phone, laptop or appliance returned to an online retailer.

This is the most powerful fact in a section 56 dispute. When a Takealot agent says "we've assessed it and it's normal wear and tear", the follow-up is:

> Please send me the assessment. Please identify the specific exclusion you're relying on, and the evidence for it. I only have to show that the item has problems, not what caused them; the onus to prove an exclusion is on the supplier (*Edan Traders v National Consumer Tribunal* [2026] ZAGPJHC 386), and a conclusion without evidence does not discharge it.

## After 6 months

Once the section 56 window closes, the onus flips back to you. If you're claiming a defect that manifested at 8 months, 18 months, or two years, you have to prove it was a *latent* defect — one that existed at the time of sale, was hidden from reasonable inspection, and renders the goods substantially less fit for purpose. You still have rights under the common-law aedilitian remedies (see [the 3-year window after the CPA runs out](/aedilitian-remedies)), but the evidence burden is harder.

Technical evidence matters. A written assessment from an independent service centre identifying the fault as a manufacturing defect (as opposed to wear or misuse) is the kind of evidence that carries weight in this window.

## Why this matters for timing

Speed matters. If your item fails, log the return inside the 6-month window even if you're on day 178. The legal weight swings dramatically on the day you cross that threshold.

## What "onus" doesn't mean

The onus point does not mean Takealot is automatically wrong. Some return claims genuinely fail on the facts — wear and tear on a product used hard for five months, a cracked screen from a drop, a PSU blown by a verifiable surge. The onus only means Takealot must *show their working*. "We decided it's wear and tear" is a conclusion, not evidence. "Our technician identified scoring on the drive consistent with physical impact, photographs attached" is evidence.

## The CGSO view

The Consumer Goods and Services Ombud's Advisory Note 1 takes a more cautious line than the courts on the burden itself. It notes that the CPA "does not say that it will be presumed that any defect that manifests within 6 months was present in the goods at the time of sale", while also recording academic commentary that "the consumer does not have to prove that the goods were unfit for their purpose when the contract was concluded."

Where the Note is firmly on the consumer's side is the inspection step. It accepts that a supplier may send goods for inspection, but only:

- "in appropriate cases, where there is a genuine reason for believing that the [supplier] may not be liable";
- if the item is inspected and returned "speedily (a maximum of 7-10 days if the item needs to be sent away)";
- and provided "the inspection and time taken thereon do not have the effect of depriving consumers of their rights under CPA."

So a routine "we'll inspect it" with no reason and no deadline sits outside what the Ombud considers acceptable. And the Note says its rules on refunds "apply irrespective of the store's refund policy or the terms of the manufacturer's guarantee/ warranty."
