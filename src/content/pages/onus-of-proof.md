# Who has to prove what

Get this right and every Takealot refusal looks different.

## Inside the first 6 months (CPA s56 window)

Return goods under section 56 of the Consumer Protection Act within 6 months of delivery and, in our reading of section 56(2), you do not have to prove the goods were defective from the moment they left the warehouse. You only have to show that they fail the section 55 quality standard *now*, while still inside the window. The cases below back that reading where problems show up soon after delivery.

The practical burden then sits with the supplier to rebut. They have to show that the defect did not exist at delivery, or that the fault came from your misuse or from an alteration made contrary to the instructions or after the goods left the supply chain's control (s 56(1)). Takealot's own exclusion list (normal wear and tear, electrical surge, consumer damage, sea-air corrosion, consumer modification, unintended use) is a list of things it has to prove, not a defence it can simply assert.

The courts have now said this, at least where problems show up soon after delivery. In *Toyota Randburg v Ndlovu* [2024] ZAGPPHC 591, a Gauteng appeal bench held that putting the burden of proving the cause on the consumer "would also be unfair on the consumer and would go against the object and purpose of the CPA especially where there is a need for in-depth knowledge that a consumer would ordinarily not have." In *Edan Traders v National Consumer Tribunal* [2026] ZAGPJHC 386, a Full Court of the Gauteng Division in Johannesburg relied on that ruling. It summarised *Toyota* as holding that where the consumer shows problems arose shortly after delivery, the supplier carries the onus to rebut the conclusion that there was a defect and to prove the problems came from the consumer's negligence: "The onus on the purchaser is to demonstrate that there were problems, not to show the cause of the problems" (para 36, citing *Toyota* para 43). And in *Wingfield Motors v National Consumer Tribunal* [2025] ZAWCHC 385, the Western Cape High Court (the forum Takealot's own T&Cs choose) rejected a dealer's claim that the customer's driving caused the defects because the dealer "did not present any evidence to support that claim but merely speculated."

All three were vehicle cases. The reasoning, that a consumer can't be expected to have the technical knowledge to prove why something failed, applies with more force to a phone, laptop or appliance returned to an online retailer.

When a Takealot agent says "we've assessed it and it's normal wear and tear", the follow-up is:

> Please send me the assessment. Please identify the specific exclusion you're relying on, and the evidence for it. I only have to show that the item has problems, not what caused them; the onus to prove an exclusion is on the supplier (*Toyota Randburg v Ndlovu* [2024] ZAGPPHC 591, relied on in *Edan Traders v National Consumer Tribunal* [2026] ZAGPJHC 386), and a conclusion without evidence does not discharge it.

## After 6 months

Once the section 56 window closes, the s 56(2) return-and-refund election is gone, although the s 55 right to goods that stay usable and durable for a reasonable period is not (s 55(2)(c) has no six-month limit). If you're claiming a defect that manifested at 8 months, 18 months, or two years, the cases above help you less: they turn on problems that showed up soon after delivery. To claim under the common-law aedilitian remedies (see [the 3-year common-law window after section 56](/aedilitian-remedies)) you have to prove a *latent* defect: one that existed at the time of sale, was hidden from reasonable inspection, and renders the goods substantially less fit for purpose. The evidence burden is harder.

Technical evidence matters. A written assessment from an independent service centre identifying the fault as a manufacturing defect (as opposed to wear or misuse) is the kind of evidence that carries weight in this window.

## Timing

Speed matters. If your item fails, log the return inside the 6-month window even if you're on day 178. The legal weight swings dramatically on the day you cross that threshold.

## What "onus" doesn't mean

The onus point does not mean Takealot is automatically wrong. Some return claims genuinely fail on the facts: wear and tear on a product used hard for five months, a cracked screen from a drop, a PSU blown by a verifiable surge. The onus only means Takealot must *show their working*. "We decided it's wear and tear" is a conclusion, not evidence. "Our technician identified scoring on the drive consistent with physical impact, photographs attached" is evidence.

## The CGSO view

The Consumer Goods and Services Ombud's Advisory Note 1 takes a more cautious line than the courts on the burden itself. It notes that the CPA "does not say that it will be presumed that any defect that manifests within 6 months was present in the goods at the time of sale", while also recording academic commentary that "the consumer does not have to prove that the goods were unfit for their purpose when the contract was concluded."

Where the Note is firmly on the consumer's side is the inspection step. It accepts that a supplier may send goods for inspection, but only:

- "in appropriate cases, where there is a genuine reason for believing that the [supplier] may not be liable";
- if the item is inspected and returned "speedily (a maximum of 7-10 days if the item needs to be sent away)";
- and provided "the inspection and time taken thereon do not have the effect of depriving consumers of their rights under CPA."

So a routine "we'll inspect it" with no reason and no deadline sits outside what the Ombud considers acceptable. And the Note says its rules on refunds "apply irrespective of the store's refund policy or the terms of the manufacturer's guarantee/ warranty."
